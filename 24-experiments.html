Make a comparison explain its denominator

A chart reports one reserve unit per survivor. That could mean four animals are still alive with one unit each, or that only one animal remains from the original four. The average is doing its arithmetic correctly, but we need more context to interpret it.

Make a comparison explain who it counts

We'll compare two maintenance costs using matched starting cohorts, then inspect the individual outcomes behind their averages. You'll implement summarize to assemble an owned report with separate cohort and survivor means, distributions, run conditions and retained-history coverage. The supplied model lets us concentrate on what that report can legitimately say.

The finished report should make a missing survivor group different from a measured zero and incomplete history different from no deaths. You'll use four paired runs to make a bounded claim about the chosen treatment, while retaining enough detail to question an appealing aggregate instead of accepting it at face value.

This isolated experiment follows four authored animals for three ticks under a chosen maintenance rule. It uses the history lesson's proposed zero-reserve terminal policy, with no feeding, movement, births or live Moss integration. That small world lets us concentrate on comparison and reporting before applying the same questions to the continuing meadow.

Ask a question the model can answer

We'll ask how increasing maintenance from one to two units per tick changes survival over three ticks. The one-unit rule is our baseline, and the two-unit rule is the treatment we're comparing against it. For the comparison to say something about that change, each pair of runs must start with the same animals and reserves. Otherwise a difference in the outcome could have come from the starting group instead.

Each starting cohort contains four stable IDs, with reserves between three and eight assigned by an explicit seeded generator. On an executed tick, the model applies any scheduled reserve grants, pays maintenance down to a floor of zero, marks zero-reserve animals dead and lets survivors increment an action counter. The main comparison schedules no grants, so its animals have only their initial reserve to spend.

Dead animals remain in the roster with zero reserve and cannot become alive again. With no births or arrivals either, the same four individuals stay identifiable throughout the run. That makes “alive out of four” a useful fixed denominator. A changing population would need a report that also accounts for who entered and left, rather than carrying this denominator over unchanged.

The birth transaction and predation resolver show some of the extra causes that appear in a richer world. Food intake, competing consumers, births and the order of actions can all affect who survives. We'll leave them out of this first reporting exercise, then take the same comparison habits back to the integrated guides, where those interactions are the point of the investigation.

Match starting conditions within each pair

For seed one, the generator supplies reserves of seven, four, four and four. Give that exact roster to both maintenance rules. If the treatment used a new sequence of draws, it might begin with more or less reserve, making its result ambiguous. Pairing the starting states removes that particular alternative explanation.

The fixture uses the inheritance example's fixed-width recurrence, but maps four successive outputs to reserves with 3 + output % 6. We name this procedure moss-cohort-lcg64-v1. To reproduce a run, keep that label and mapping together with its seed, horizon, maintenance cost, initial policy and any pending interventions. The seed alone doesn't specify the experiment.

The tests check matching before they check outcomes. At every chosen seed, they compare the canonical initial animal states used for the two costs. They then compare the completed runs with literal expected differences. These checks answer separate questions: did we start the pair fairly, and did the requested cost actually affect the executed model? A simulation that ignored the treatment could pass the first check while failing the second.

A matching roster is necessary, but current reserve isn't the whole state. Suppose two runs show identical animals while only one has a reserve grant queued for the next tick. They can diverge without any further change to the maintenance rule. The canonical snapshot therefore includes pending grants, the completed tick, identity, reserve, life and action count. Tests expose both the queued-input difference and a clock difference between otherwise identical all-dead rosters. Camera position stays outside this model, while the experiment's specification must still be compared alongside its snapshot.

Repeat the pair before generalizing

One matched pair can explain its own difference; it can't show how that difference varies with a new starting cohort. Repeat the matched comparison for seeds one through four while keeping the treatment and horizon fixed. Record treatment survivors minus baseline survivors for each pair, giving minus three, minus two, minus two and minus two. Keeping those four differences lets us see how the result varies with the starting cohort, rather than replacing them immediately with a single average.

What we can say is that, under the stated rules and horizon, the higher cost reduced survival in each of these four matched runs.

The direction of that result also follows from the simple model: spending more can't add reserve, there is no feeding to compensate, and death stops later actions. Running several fixtures checks that we implemented the relationship consistently. It doesn't turn that relationship into a surprising ecological discovery. In the continuing meadow, where a behavioral change can affect both costs and access to food, interpreting the result will require more of the individual evidence.

Keep the denominator and distribution

For seed one's treatment, the final sorted reserves are zero, zero, zero and one. Our summarize function will report two means: the cohort mean divides the total reserve of one by all four original members, while the survivor mean divides one by the single remaining animal. Both are legitimate calculations, but only the first retains the original cohort as its denominator. Labeling the group is part of reporting the number.

The mean type keeps an integer sum and count, postponing division rather than rounding to a floating-point result immediately. It also represents an empty group with None. If all four animals die, the cohort still has four members and a known mean of zero over four; the survivor group has no members and no mean. Substituting zero for that absence would make two different situations look like the same measurement.

Even a correctly labeled mean can hide a distribution. Compare reserves of zero, zero, zero, eight with two, two, two, two: each cohort averages two, but their individual outcomes differ sharply. Filtering the first group to its positive member would report eight and hide three zeros altogether. We'll therefore keep the sorted reserve values and owned individual outcomes so an aggregate can be examined rather than merely accepted.

Owning those outcomes also makes the report a stable record of the run. You can follow an unexpected average back to a named individual without keeping the world borrowed, and later changes to the original run won't rewrite the saved result. The report carries its own specification and collection boundary so its values can be understood in the context where they were observed.

Coverage is part of the result

Individual history introduces one more kind of missing information. The death journal uses the retained-coverage rule: when a journal with two slots receives a third record, it evicts the oldest. If even one record from a tick is lost, a query for the complete event count at that tick must report that it cannot answer, although other records from the tick may remain.

Seed one's treatment makes this concrete because three animals become terminal on tick two. A two-slot journal retains two records and evicts one, leaving complete history only from tick three onward. Asking it for a complete count over the whole three-tick experiment therefore returns unknown. Keeping two visible records isn't enough to reconstruct the missing third event.

The roster still tells us something useful: three members of this fixed cohort are dead. That present-day census doesn't recover all the lost event details, but incomplete history doesn't erase the census either. Keeping the two sources distinct lets the report say what is known about the ending population while remaining honest about which transitions it can explain.

A test changes the retention limit from one slot to eight and checks that canonical biology stays the same. The journal should affect how much evidence we retain, rather than changing what the animals do. A later browser chart or exporter will need to preserve that distinction too; the native test establishes it for this model.

Predict the report, then build it

Before implementing the report, predict both means for seed one's treatment and decide whether its two-slot journal can count the full interval. Then raise maintenance to twenty, making all four animals die on the first tick. Consider what changes in each denominator: does the absence of survivors also remove the original cohort?

Hint 1: choose a population before counting

Build the two groups deliberately. The cohort includes the dead members retained in the fixed roster, while the survivor group includes only animals whose current life state is alive. Each mean needs its own sum and count.

Hint 2: require complete collection and retention

The journal can answer only if collection has reached the requested horizon and every tick in the requested interval remains complete. An unexecuted run hasn't collected evidence for its future ticks, even if its journal is currently empty.

Prediction answer

For seed one, the cohort mean is one over four and the survivor mean is one over one. The two-slot journal can't give a complete event count for the full interval. When all four die, the cohort remains a group of four with mean zero, while the empty survivor group has no mean.

use std::collections::VecDeque;
const ALGORITHM: &str = "moss-cohort-lcg64-v1";
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
struct Spec { seed: u64, maintenance_units: u32, ticks: u32, history_limit: usize }
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
struct Animal { id: u32, reserve: u32, alive: bool, actions: u32 }
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq, PartialOrd, Ord)]
struct Grant { tick: u32, id: u32, units: u32 }
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
struct Death { tick: u32, id: u32 }
#[derive(Clone, Debug)]
struct Run {
    spec: Spec,
    animals: Vec<Animal>,
    pending: Vec<Grant>,
    deaths: VecDeque<Death>,
    evicted: usize,
    complete_from: u32,
    collected_through: u32,
}
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
struct Mean { sum: u64, count: usize }
#[derive(Clone, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
struct Report {
    spec: Spec,
    algorithm: &'static str,
    outcomes: Vec<Animal>,
    pending: Vec<Grant>,
    initial: usize,
    survivors: usize,
    sorted_reserves: Vec<u32>,
    cohort_mean: Option<Mean>,
    survivor_mean: Option<Mean>,
    complete_death_count: Option<usize>,
    retained_deaths: Vec<Death>,
    complete_from: u32,
    collected_through: u32,
    evicted: usize,
}
fn mean(values: impl Iterator<Item = u32>) -> Option<Mean> {
    let (sum, count) = values.fold((0_u64, 0_usize), |(sum, count), value|
        (sum.checked_add(u64::from(value)).expect("sum exhausted"),
         count.checked_add(1).expect("count exhausted")));
    (count > 0).then_some(Mean { sum, count })
}
fn initial(spec: Spec) -> Run {
    assert!(spec.history_limit > 0);
    let mut state = spec.seed;
    let animals = (1..=4).map(|id| {
        state = state.wrapping_mul(6_364_136_223_846_793_005)
            .wrapping_add(1_442_695_040_888_963_407);
        Animal { id, reserve: 3 + ((state >> 32) as u32 % 6), alive: true, actions: 0 }
    }).collect();
    Run { spec, animals, pending: vec![], deaths: VecDeque::new(), evicted: 0,
        complete_from: 1, collected_through: 0 }
}
fn advance(run: &mut Run) {
    let tick = run.collected_through.checked_add(1).expect("tick exhausted");
    assert!(tick <= run.spec.ticks);
    for grant in run.pending.iter().filter(|grant| grant.tick == tick) {
        if let Some(animal) = run.animals.iter_mut().find(|animal| animal.id == grant.id && animal.alive) {
            animal.reserve = animal.reserve.checked_add(grant.units).expect("grant exceeds units");
        }
    }
    run.pending.retain(|grant| grant.tick > tick);
    for animal in run.animals.iter_mut().filter(|animal| animal.alive) {
        animal.reserve = animal.reserve.saturating_sub(run.spec.maintenance_units);
        if animal.reserve == 0 {
            animal.alive = false;
            if run.deaths.len() == run.spec.history_limit {
                let removed = run.deaths.pop_front().unwrap();
                run.complete_from = run.complete_from.max(removed.tick.checked_add(1).unwrap());
                run.evicted = run.evicted.checked_add(1).unwrap();
            }
            run.deaths.push_back(Death { tick, id: animal.id });
        } else {
            animal.actions = animal.actions.checked_add(1).unwrap();
        }
    }
    run.collected_through = tick;
}
fn execute(spec: Spec) -> Run {
    let mut run = initial(spec);
    for _ in 0..spec.ticks { advance(&mut run); }
    run
}

fn canonical(run: &Run) -> (u32, Vec<Animal>, Vec<Grant>) {
    let mut animals = run.animals.clone(); animals.sort_by_key(|animal| animal.id);
    let mut pending = run.pending.clone(); pending.sort();
    (run.collected_through, animals, pending)
}
fn spec(seed: u64, maintenance_units: u32, history_limit: usize) -> Spec {
    Spec { seed, maintenance_units, ticks: 3, history_limit }
}
fn main() {
    println!("algorithm={ALGORITHM} ticks=3 seeds=1,2,3,4");
    for seed in 1..=4 {
        let baseline = summarize(&execute(spec(seed, 1, 2)));
        let treatment = summarize(&execute(spec(seed, 2, 2)));
        println!("seed={seed} alive={}/{} -> {}/{} delta={}", baseline.survivors,
            baseline.initial, treatment.survivors, treatment.initial,
            treatment.survivors as i64 - baseline.survivors as i64);
    }
    let run = execute(spec(1, 2, 2));
    let report = summarize(&run);
    println!("seed=1 treatment reserves={:?} cohort={:?} survivors={:?}",
        report.sorted_reserves, report.cohort_mean, report.survivor_mean);
    println!("journal death count={:?} retained={} evicted={} complete={}..={}",
        report.complete_death_count, report.retained_deaths.len(), report.evicted,
        report.complete_from, report.collected_through);
    println!("pending after horizon={}", canonical(&run).2.len());
}

        assert_eq!(report.initial, 4);
        assert_eq!(report.sorted_reserves, vec![0, 0, 0, 1]);

        assert_eq!((report.initial, report.survivors), (4, 0));
        assert_eq!(report.survivor_mean, None);
        assert_eq!(report.cohort_mean, Some(Mean { sum: 0, count: 4 }));
        assert_eq!(report.complete_death_count, Some(4));

    #[test]
    fn pending_interventions_are_part_of_state_and_change_future_outcomes() {

fn summarize(run: &Run) -> Report {
    let (_, outcomes, pending) = canonical(run);
    let mut sorted_reserves: Vec<_> = run.animals.iter().map(|animal| animal.reserve).collect();
    sorted_reserves.sort_unstable();
    let full_coverage = run.complete_from == 1 && run.collected_through == run.spec.ticks
        && run.collected_through > 0;
    Report { spec: run.spec, algorithm: ALGORITHM, outcomes, pending, initial: run.animals.len(), survivors: run.animals.iter().filter(|animal| animal.alive).count(),
        sorted_reserves, cohort_mean: mean(run.animals.iter().map(|animal| animal.reserve)),
        survivor_mean: mean(run.animals.iter().filter(|animal| animal.alive).map(|animal| animal.reserve)),
        complete_death_count: full_coverage.then_some(run.deaths.len()),
        retained_deaths: run.deaths.iter().copied().collect(), complete_from: run.complete_from,
        collected_through: run.collected_through, evicted: run.evicted }
}

            treatment.survivors as i64 - baseline.survivors as i64);
    }
    let run = execute(spec(1, 2, 2));
    let report = summarize(&run);
    println!("seed=1 treatment reserves={:?} cohort={:?} survivors={:?}",
        report.sorted_reserves, report.cohort_mean, report.survivor_mean);

Make a claim the evidence supports

The opening average becomes interpretable when we name its denominator, retain the individual outcomes and state what history is missing. Across our four matched pairs, higher maintenance leaves fewer survivors under the specified rules and horizon. That is a supported comparison of these cases. Before calling a setup better for a game or an ecosystem, decide what that claim means and what evidence beyond these survival counts could support it.

Recall answers

The average alone doesn't reveal how many original members survived or how their reserves are distributed. One cohort can also hide sensitivity to starting conditions, while four chosen cohorts still don't settle every scenario. Enjoyment needs a player-facing question beyond survival, and these runs contain no reproduction or inherited change across generations from which to infer evolution.

The tests establish the declared toy behavior and report contracts. Evaluating a scenario's balance, connecting it to a browser chart and judging the experience of watching it are further investigations with different evidence. A sound report helps with all of them because it exposes its denominator and gaps instead of quietly making those decisions for the reader.
