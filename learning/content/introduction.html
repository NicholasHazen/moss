Moss is a browser-based ecosystem sandbox: a small world whose inhabitants live by rules you can inspect and change. The ambition is to follow an animal toward food, notice another arrive first, and understand how that encounter changes what happens next. Building the world gives us a reason to learn Rust; learning Rust gives us more control over the world we can build.

Meet the world before changing its rules

This introduction explains what Moss is aiming for, what you can already build with Fieldnotes, and how its language and tools fit together. We'll begin with an individual creature, widen the view to its surroundings, and then connect those ideas to Rust, Bevy ECS, and the browser. You don't need to know those tools yet. You should leave with a picture of the project and a reason for the first, deliberately small exercise.

A small life worth following

One of our example animals is a hare nicknamed Fern. Giving her a name makes a particular life easier to follow: we can ask why Fern stopped beside a patch while another hare kept moving. Her nickname doesn't determine her behavior. The program keeps her identity, species, position, and condition as separate facts, so the same rules can apply to many individuals without hard-coding a story for each one.

Imagine selecting her in the browser. You can see where she is, how much reserve she has left, what she observed nearby, and where she intends to go. Reserve is our simple numerical account of what an animal can spend on staying alive and acting. A trip toward food can reduce that account before a meal replenishes it. Watching the number alone is less interesting than connecting it to the journey that caused the change.

Widen the view and those individual decisions begin to interact. A hungry grazer can empty a patch another animal was approaching. A rest can delay arrival. A birth adds a new consumer, and a hunter can remove a possible parent. We want to pause at such moments, inspect the participants, change a starting condition, and try again. A population collapse can be worth investigating when the world gives us enough evidence to explain it.

Why begin with a grid?

The first spaces in Moss are finite, two-dimensional grids. An animal's position is a cell with horizontal and vertical coordinates. That makes an early movement rule concrete: propose the next cell, check that it lies inside the world, and decide whether the animal can pay to reach it. We can trace every accepted step without introducing continuous motion and collision geometry at the same time.

Cells also give the first food interactions a clear boundary. Under the course's chosen rules, a grazer must occupy the selected patch's cell before it can eat. Several animals may share a cell, so arriving together still leaves competition to resolve. A square in the display is a schematic marker; its drawn size isn't the animal's physical reach. This keeps camera zoom from accidentally changing who can eat.

The grid is a starting representation, and the longer vision leaves room for chambers, routes, habitats, and richer terrain. More detailed shapes, continuous positions, obstacles, or different movement rules would be design choices with consequences to test. We don't need to settle them before a first animal becomes interesting. Keeping space, biological rules, and drawing separate makes those later choices easier to examine without confusing an attractive picture with a changed world.

Make the consequences of a rule visible

A simulation advances its state according to rules. In Moss, we group an ordered round of that work into a tick. A tick might charge upkeep, grow food, let eligible animals act, and record the result. Which operations belong in it, and their order, are decisions we write down and test. Checking for starvation before a possible meal describes a different opportunity from checking afterward.

This is where the project becomes more than a collection of moving shapes. Two runs might end with the same number of animals even though one had many more births and deaths. A patch might contain food after the animals that needed it have died. Current state tells part of the story; recorded events and comparisons help recover the rest. The course uses explicit game rules to make these relationships inspectable, rather than treating familiar species names as evidence of biological realism.

Use Rust to make changes deliberate

Rust is the programming language in which we'll express those rules. If you've programmed before, functions, conditionals, loops, and data structures will feel familiar. The distinctive work is often deciding who owns a value and what access another part of the program needs. Rust's compiler checks ownership and borrowing relationships, catching many invalid uses before the program runs. The official ownership explanation provides a fuller account when you want it.

For Moss, that question arises as soon as an inspector reads Fern's reserve. Does it need temporary access to the current value, or a saved measurement it can keep after she moves on? If a meal changes both the animal and the patch, where should those changes happen? We'll learn the language through decisions like these, so a compiler error points back to a relationship we can explain.

The compiler won't decide whether two units is a fair travel price or whether a feeding rule makes a compelling world. Types and checked arithmetic help express the policy precisely; tests and experiments ask whether the program applies it and what follows. That combination is the reason to use Rust here: clear control over data and change, with an increasingly useful account of what the running program actually did.

Let Bevy ECS organize the inhabitants and their rules

Bevy is a game engine written in Rust. Its entity-component-system library, usually shortened to ECS, can also be used on its own. We use that library to organize simulation state and the functions that work on it. You don't need a visual editor or a running window to test those functions; the Bevy ECS reference for this edition describes the same library used by the course.

An entity identifies a thing in the world. Fern is one entity; a food patch is another. A component is a piece of data attached to an entity, such as its position or reserve. Different entities can carry different combinations: a patch needs a biomass amount, while an animal needs a reserve. Their names don't force both into one enormous record containing every field any inhabitant might someday need.

A system is a function that requests the data needed for a job. An upkeep system asks for the animals' reserves and the rates it should charge. A query describes which component combinations it can visit and whether it may read or change them. A schedule then arranges when systems run. These are tools for expressing our rules; Bevy doesn't supply a hare's diet or decide whether a newborn may act immediately.

Within a running simulation, one world owns the authoritative state. An inspector can copy a reading from it, and a graph can summarize several readings, without either becoming another simulation whose reserves must be kept in sync. That distinction will let us test the rules directly and examine their consequences through several views.

Give the simulation and the browser different jobs

The browser is where we want to watch and investigate Moss. Rust can compile to WebAssembly, a portable code format that browsers can execute alongside their normal web interfaces. A small host connects controls and displayed readings to that compiled program. You can find the format's background on the official WebAssembly site; understanding its instruction set isn't a prerequisite for our lessons.

The useful distinction is between advancing the world and drawing it. Pressing Step requests simulation work. Selecting an animal or redrawing a label asks to see what is already there. Looking more often mustn't make Fern spend more reserve. The same Rust rules can also run in native tests without a browser, giving us a way to investigate them before asking whether a button and its display behave correctly.

Know where the working route begins and ends

The original Moss application currently provides a deliberately small foundation: Fern the hare, a fox nicknamed Flint, and a grass patch named Meadow. Meadow is that patch's name, not the entire map. The animals pay upkeep on each executed tick. The prepared movement rule remains the active learner assignment, so the opening browser scene is stationary. The first lesson uses that small state change to make observation and mutation easy to distinguish.

The continuing Fieldnotes project goes much further. In a saved practice directory, you'll connect finite food and growth to survival, add funded births and inherited upkeep, then combine local observations, paid travel, rest, and hunting. The guides include complete reference implementations and compiled browser views through hunting. The final refuge extension asks you to design a protection rule and investigate placements with native tests and reports.

These are two clearly named contexts in the course: the original application preserves its current hands-on assignment, while the course project gives you a complete route for building and studying richer interactions. Extending your practice project doesn't silently install its rules in the original application. Beyond the delivered route, Moss's wider ambition remains open: more varied habitats and behavior should grow from something you want to understand or change in the world you've made.

Keep the world familiar as your responsibilities grow

Fieldnotes is your way into this project; you don't need to read an older Moss book first. The early lessons use small examples so you can concentrate on one Rust or ECS decision. You'll predict a result, make an edit or investigate a diagnostic, and compare what happened with what you expected. Hints and full answers stay available, including the reasoning behind them.

Once the pieces are familiar, the continuing meadow gives them a shared home. You keep the same project as the guides add interactions, reconstruct whole systems, and write tests for questions the supplied checks leave open. By the refuge capstone, you choose both how a feature belongs in the program and which experimental claim to challenge. The learning path shows where the smaller lessons support that work.

You can read and listen before installing anything. Narration follows the prose, while code, diagrams, and worked answers remain available on the page; pause to inspect them when a lesson asks you to predict or edit. When you want the compiler's feedback, the local workbench keeps the course beside your editor. Optional later chapters return to lifetimes, shared ownership, concurrency, and macros when a concrete question gives those subjects a purpose.

Begin with one change you can explain

The world we're building should reward a closer look: an individual's journey should connect to a rule you can find, an outcome you can test, and a wider pattern you can investigate. Starting with a small grid and a few clear rules gives us room to learn those connections before making the world richer.

Begin with a tick and an observation. Fern won't travel yet, but her reserve will change. Explaining that first difference establishes the boundary every later journey, meal, and birth will rely on.
