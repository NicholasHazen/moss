Learn Rust by making a little life explain itself.

Fern begins with 60 units of energy and a patch of grass in the distance. One executed tick changes her reserve; drawing another frame doesn't. Start by explaining that small difference, then use the same habit as the world grows: predict what should happen, write the Rust that makes it happen, and choose evidence that could show you were wrong.

Fieldnotes is a new learning format alongside the existing Moss book. Each lesson develops a question through explanation, a small experiment, and a runnable Rust example. You can read straight through, listen while the current passage is highlighted, or save a note for another visit. Hints and complete answers are available whenever you want them.

Keep one meadow as you learn. After the query, schedule, meal, and growth lessons, bring the pieces into a continuing project. Reconstruct whole ECS systems, write an investigation, and inspect your compiled source in the browser. Extend that saved project with funded births and descendants, give those animals local choices and paid journeys, then let fatigue interrupt a journey and hunters compete for prey. In the refuge capstone, choose how shelter belongs in that world, implement the complete system, and test your own prediction about its placement. Your earlier work stays in the same project; native tests and experiments let you investigate the final extension.

Your live assignment remains yours. NOW.md in the Moss checkout is still the source of truth for active project work. The course labs and continuing meadow are separate from that live world, so working through them doesn't replace or complete the movement task.

Begin with something you can inspect

A language learned through consequences

The opening lessons ask who owns a value and who may change it. Optional data, explicit errors, collections, and system order become useful as the world poses further questions: what if the target is gone, or two animals want the last unit of food? We'll slow down for the Rust reasoning behind those decisions while keeping familiar programming syntax brief.

The fieldnotes lead from that first tick through ecological experiments and selected advanced Rust. Lifetimes help explain borrowed observations; concurrency raises the question of when an arriving result may change the world. The learning path shows those connections and where to join the continuing project. On a later visit, return with a different question to practice choosing evidence in a changed fixture.

Connect the rule, the schedule, and the view

A helper test can show that a meal calculation handles its stated cases. An ECS integration test can show that the intended animals actually receive that meal in the installed schedule. A browser observation can then show what the compiled application exposes through its controls. We'll use all three, keeping clear which part of the explanation each supports.

Open the local workbench when you're ready to run Rust. Reading needs only this static website: there is no account, and optional notes and progress stay in your browser. You can move between the explanation and your editor without giving up the diagrams, narration, or saved place that make a longer investigation easier to follow.
