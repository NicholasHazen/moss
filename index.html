A living world. An inspectable program.

A world worth understanding.

Build a living ecosystem with Rust and Bevy ECS.

Moss is an ecosystem sandbox for the browser. We’re building a world where small creatures find food, rest, compete, reproduce, and leave consequences for the lives around them.

Meet Moss →Returning? Find your place in the learning path.

You can pause at something surprising, inspect what happened, change a rule, and try again. The world should become worth exploring as your ability to build it grows.

A small meadow gives us somewhere to begin. Food can return after a hungry animal has already gone, two creatures can reach the same patch, and rest can delay an animal's arrival. Those interactions give us questions to investigate before we widen the world into more varied habitats, routes, or a dungeon ecology. The grid used by the first examples keeps early decisions easy to trace; it doesn't settle what every future landscape must become.

Fieldnotes is the learning guide to that project. It teaches practical Rust and the entity-component-system approach used to organize Moss, through a world you can gradually make your own. You don't need to know those tools or read an earlier Moss book before starting.

Keep the world familiar as the questions grow

The opening lessons use small, complete examples to make Rust's ownership, borrowing, and data modelling decisions visible. You might keep an old reading while the world changes, explain why a query skipped an animal, or write a test that reveals an observer ran too early. Predictions, staged hints, and full worked answers give you room to reason before comparing your approach.

Later, those pieces cooperate in one saved course project. You reconstruct whole systems and extend the same meadow through finite food, births and descendants, local choices, paid travel, rest, and hunting. Your earlier tests stay with the world as it changes. The final refuge capstone asks you to design a shelter rule and challenge your own claim about its effect, using the implementation and evidence you've learned to build.

Choose where to join the work

  1. Meet the project. Start with the world, its inhabitants, and why Rust, Bevy, and the browser fit together. The introduction distinguishes the small original application from the fuller course world you'll go on to build.
  2. Explain one change. Begin the lessons with a tick and an observation, then follow the language and ECS ideas through experiments with concrete outcomes.
  3. Keep one meadow growing. Join the continuing project once the core, meal, and growth work is familiar. Return to its guides in the same saved directory as your responsibilities expand.

Make understanding part of the result

A meal that appears to work is a beginning. We also want to know that it took the right amount from the patch, reached the intended animal, and appeared correctly in the browser. The lessons connect small function tests, checks of installed systems, and actual browser observations, so you can tell which part of an explanation each result supports.

Read here, listen with the current passage highlighted, or keep a note for another visit. The reading guide explains those tools; the local workbench brings the compiler beside the lesson when you're ready to edit. You can also return with a different question to revisit an idea under a changed condition.
